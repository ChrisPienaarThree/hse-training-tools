    Stiffness and the joint diagram

    kb = AdAtE / (Adlt + Atld)  ·  C = kb / (kb + km)  ·  Fb = Fi + C P  ·  Fm = Fi − (1 − C) P

    Tightening stretches the bolt and squeezes the members by the same force. An external load then stretches the bolt a little more and lets the members relax by the same amount, so the load is shared in the ratio of the stiffnesses. Because the members are usually five to ten times stiffer than the bolt, the bolt sees only a small fraction C of the external load — until the members are fully unloaded and the joint separates.

    Member stiffness

    km = 0.5774 π E d / ln[(1.155 t + 0.5 d)(2.5 d) / ((1.155 t + 2.5 d)(0.5 d))] per frustum

    The clamped material acts as a pair of 30° cones of pressure under the head and nut. A soft gasket in series with those cones collapses the member stiffness, raises C and hands most of the load to the bolt — good for sealing, bad for bolt fatigue.

    Torque and tension

    T = K Fi d  ·  Fi ≈ 0.75 Fp (reusable)  ·  Fp = Sp At

    Roughly 90 % of the wrench torque is spent on thread and face friction; only the rest stretches the bolt. K bundles that friction. It changes with plating, oil and dirt, and it scatters ±25–35 % between nominally identical bolts, so torque gives preload only approximately. A torque set for dry threads on a lubricated bolt can push the bolt past proof. Turn-of-nut and stretch methods bypass friction and cut the scatter.

    Embedding

    ΔF = fz · kbkm / (kb + km)

    After tightening, the microscopic peaks on every interface flatten under load, releasing a few micrometres of the joint's stretch. In a short, stiff joint a few micrometres are a large share of the total elongation, so preload sags — more so with paint, rough surfaces or gaskets. Re-torquing after bedding-in, or longer bolts, are the usual answers.

    Separation, yield and fatigue

    P0 = Fi / (1 − nC)  ·  np = Fp / (Fi + nC P)  ·  nf = Se(Su − σi) / [σa(Su + Se)]

    Preload must be high enough to keep the joint closed at the maximum load, yet low enough that the bolt does not pass its proof load. With a cyclic load the bolt alternating stress is only the C-share of the load range, which is why a well-preloaded stiff joint almost never fatigues and a loose or gasketed one does. The load introduction factor n accounts for where the load enters the clamped stack.

    About the numbers

    [Illustrative data, for training only.] Proof, yield and tensile strengths and the rolled-thread endurance limits follow ISO property classes; torque coefficients and their scatter are typical values; the scatter is drawn from a triangular distribution; gasket moduli are apparent values; embedding amounts are typical per interface. Real joints are validated by measurement.

    How to use the bench

    • Build the joint: bolt size and class, grip, member material, gasket and surface condition. The readouts show stiffnesses and C at once.
    • Tighten: choose the method and lubrication, set the torque (or the target preload) and press Tighten. Each tightening draws a random K from the scatter band; Tighten 20 bolts fills the torque–tension chart with dots.
    • Load it: set the maximum and minimum load and switch the cyclic load on. Watch the joint diagram partition the load, the clamp fall, the separation and proof margins, and the bolt fatigue factor.
    • Embedding sags the preload in the first minute after tightening; speed it up with the clock. Record readings to compare joints in the tasks.
