    Stress concentration

    σpeak = Kt σnom  ·  q = 1 / (1 + a/r)  ·  Kf = 1 + q (Kt − 1)

    A fillet, hole or groove raises the local stress by the geometric factor Kt, which depends only on shape (r/d and D/d). Under cyclic load the material does not feel the whole of it: the notch sensitivity q, which rises with strength and with notch radius, gives the fatigue factor Kf. Sharp notches in high-strength steel are felt almost fully; blunt notches in soft steel are partly forgiven.

    Endurance limit and Marin factors

    Se = ka kb kc ke Se′  ·  Se′ ≈ 0.5 Su (steel)

    The polished laboratory specimen sets Se′. The real part is derated for surface finish (ka, worst for forged and hot-rolled surfaces), size (kb), load type (kc, axial 0.85) and reliability (ke). Aluminium has no knee: its S-N line keeps falling, so a "fatigue strength" is quoted at 5×10⁸ cycles.

    S-N curve

    Sf = a Nb  ·  a = (f Su)² / Se  ·  b = −⅓ log(f Su / Se)  ·  N = (σar / a)^{1/b}

    Between 10³ and 10⁶ cycles the strength falls as a straight line on log-log axes. Because the slope is shallow, a small change in stress makes a large change in life: halving the stress amplitude near the knee can multiply life a hundredfold. That is why radius, finish and mean stress matter so much.

    Mean stress

    Goodman: σa/Se + σm/Su = 1/n  ·  Gerber: σa/Se + (σm/Su)² = 1/n  ·  Langer: σa + σm = Sy/n

    A tensile mean stress uses up part of the fatigue strength. The criteria differ in how much: Goodman is linear and conservative, Gerber fits test data through the middle, Soderberg is safest, ASME-elliptic sits between. The equivalent completely reversed stress σar converts the (σm, σa) pair into the stress the S-N curve understands. Yield is checked separately: if Kf·σmax exceeds Sy the notch yields and the mean stress relaxes.

    Crack growth

    da/dN = C (ΔK)^m  ·  ΔK = Y Δσ √(πa)  ·  ac = (Kc / Y σmax)² / π

    Most of a notched part's life is spent making a crack; once a crack exists the Paris law grows it faster and faster until it reaches the critical length and the ligament fails. A visible crack usually means little life remains. Only the tensile part of the cycle opens the crack.

    About the numbers

    [Illustrative data, for training only.] Kt curve fits follow the usual stepped-shaft and plate-with-hole charts; Peterson's constant a = 0.0254 (2070/Su)^1.8 mm for steel and 0.5 mm for aluminium; Marin factors per the textbook tables; the low-cycle region below 10³ cycles is simplified; Paris constants are typical class values and the crack-growth phase is compressed into the S-N life when it would exceed it.

    How to use the bench

    • Choose a stepped shaft (bending or axial) or a plate with a hole, then the material, dimensions, fillet radius or hole size, finish and reliability.
    • Load it: amplitude and mean, the criterion, the frequency and the cycle clock. The readouts give Kt, q, Kf, Se, the notch stresses, factors of safety and the predicted life.
    • Start cycling: the damage bar fills, the crack initiates at the notch (drawn magnified), grows along the Paris curve and the specimen fractures. New specimen resets the clock.
    • Charts: the S-N diagram with the smooth-specimen and derated curves, the Goodman diagram with all criteria, and the crack-growth curve. Recorded readings appear as grey dots.
