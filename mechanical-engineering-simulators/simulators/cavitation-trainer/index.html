    What NPSH actually is

    NPSHa = patm/ρg + z − hsuction losses − pvapour/ρg

    The absolute pressure head at the pump eye, above the liquid's vapour pressure. Anything that lowers eye pressure (suction lift, a throttled suction line, a fouled strainer) or raises vapour pressure (temperature) eats into it. The pump needs a minimum, NPSHr, which rises with flow and with the square of speed.

    Inception before the head drops

    Bubbles start forming and collapsing while the margin is still positive: you hear the crackle and see the accelerometer floor rise well before the head–flow performance changes. NPSH3, the value at which head has fallen 3 %, is a test convention, not the start of damage. Damage rates are often highest at margins just above NPSH3, where bubbles are still collapsing on metal instead of filling the impeller.

    Why it is loud and why it vibrates

    Each vapour bubble collapses in microseconds, driving a liquid micro-jet at the surface. Thousands per second give the gravel-in-the-pump sound and a broadband, random vibration signature. A mechanical fault gives a discrete peak; cavitation raises the whole floor.

    Telling the four damage mechanisms apart

    Cavitation: sponge-like pitting on the suction (low-pressure) side of the vane leading edge, near the eye; clean metal elsewhere; history of low NPSH margin. Suction recirculation: similar pitting but on the pressure side of the vane inlet and on the shroud at the eye; history of running well below BEP with NPSH margin fine. Erosion: smooth, polished, directional grooves following the flow, thinned vane tips; abrasive solids in the fluid. Corrosion: uniform roughening and pitting on all wetted surfaces including ones the flow never scours, discolouration, aggressive fluid chemistry.

    How to use the trainer

    • Enable sound (top right) so the pump hum and the cavitation crackle play through the speakers.
    • Set a test flow with the discharge valve, then reduce NPSHa progressively: close the suction throttle, warm the fluid, or lower the liquid level.
    • Watch everything at once: the vacuum gauge falls, bubbles appear at the impeller eye, the microphone level and accelerometer RMS rise, and finally the head and flow drop. The time strip shows the order in which they happen.
    • Record points as you go, or auto-run the test, to build the classic head-versus-NPSHa curve and find NPSH3.
    • Diagnose the impellers in the drawer: four samples, four mechanisms, evidence cards, feedback.
