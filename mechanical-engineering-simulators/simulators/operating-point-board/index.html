    Print set

    The print/ folder next to this page holds the physical board, generated from the same model file: board-A1 (the chart, 841 × 594 mm), pieces-A1 sheets (pump, speed, impeller and parallel curves, system parabolas — all at the board's scale), pieces-small-A3 (POR/AOR bars, BEP dots, ? and ✓ markers, static-head lines), scenario-cards (A5, two per A4), facilitator-key and team-record. PDF and SVG versions of each.

    Making it

    Print everything at 100 %. Mount the board on a steel sheet or a magnetic whiteboard. Laminate the piece sheets, cut on the black outline, and run magnetic tape along the back of each strip. Each pump piece carries its Q = 0 end and white ticks at Q = 10, 20, 30 for registration; each system piece is a parabola relative to the static head, so its lower end sits on the H = Hs grid line.

    Running it

    Card 0 commissions the board, then A to D one at a time, E and F for fast teams. Teams register the pieces, mark the BEP, lay the POR/AOR bar, place the red ? where the curves cross and write down Q, H, % BEP, POR yes/no, η and kW. Then verify here (Projector mode hides the digital pieces so the screen only reveals the answer) or on the hydraulic loop bench.

    Why the pieces move the way they do

    The pump curve belongs to the machine: speed and impeller diameter scale it by the affinity laws, nothing else moves it. The system curve belongs to the plant: static head sets where it starts and friction sets how steeply it climbs. The pump runs where the two meet, and only there.

    Q ∝ N·D  ·  H ∝ (N·D)²  ·  P ∝ (N·D)³  ·  Hsys = Hs + K·Q²

    Strainer, tank, valve, speed

    A fouling strainer and a closing valve both raise K: the parabola steepens, the point slides up and left along the pump curve. A falling supply tank raises the static head: the parabola moves up unchanged, same direction of travel, usually a bigger loss of flow. A VFD shrinks the pump curve instead: the point moves down and left along the system curve, which is why the head falls with the flow and the power drops so much further than with the valve.

    POR and AOR

    The preferred operating region (70–120 % of QBEP, ANSI/HI 9.6.3) is where vibration, bearing loads and recirculation stay low; the allowable region is wider and set by the manufacturer (50–130 % here, illustrative). Both scale with speed and diameter because QBEP does — which is why every pump piece has its own bar.

    Two pumps in parallel

    Add flows at equal head. On a steep system the combined curve meets the parabola not far to the right of the single-pump point, so each pump backs off to the left of its POR. Parallel pumping pays on flat, friction-light systems.

    Power

    P = ρ g Q H / η  (Q in m³/s)

    Throttling to 18 m³/h leaves the pump making 25 m of head to deliver 16.5 m of useful work; the valve destroys the rest. The VFD at 1 200 rpm delivers about the same flow at 15.6 m for close to half the power.

    About the data

    [Illustrative pump and system data, for training only.] The pump is the same fictional 4 kW unit used on the hydraulic loop bench and the pump trainer: H = 32 − 0.2Q − 0.01Q² at 1 480 rpm, BEP 24 m³/h, η 72 %. Baseline system: static head 10 m, K = 0.020 (pipe 0.015 + clean strainer 0.005).

    How to use the board

    • Pick a scenario card on the right. It says which pieces you need; those buttons in the tray are outlined in brass.
    • Add pieces from the tray and drag them onto the chart. Pump curves and bars snap when you register them to the grid (Q = 0 end, white ticks). System parabolas snap to Q = 0 and slide up and down: their lower end sits on the static head.
    • Predict: drag the red ? marker to where the curves cross. Its Q and H show on the right. Press Check: the true curves and the true point appear and the result is logged for the team.
    • Projector mode hides the digital pieces so the screen only reveals the answer for teams working on the magnetic board.
