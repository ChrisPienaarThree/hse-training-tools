Machine train

Bolt a mass to the disc and watch the 1× peak appear. Then find the correction mass that cancels it — single-plane balancing with the tachometer as the phase reference.

Shim the motor feet wrong. Angular misalignment shows as axial 1× with 2×, 180° out of phase across the coupling; offset shows as radial 2×.

Back off the hold-down bolts on bearing 2, or fit a bent shaft. Looseness gives a comb of harmonics and half-order lines; a bent shaft gives axial 1× with the two ends 180° apart.

Fit a damaged 6205 bearing. Defect impacts ring the bearing's own resonance around 3.5 kHz; the raw spectrum shows a haystack there and the envelope spectrum shows the defect frequency lines.

Fit the gearbox (23 : 57 teeth) or the belt drive (120 : 240 mm pulleys, 1.5 m belt) using the checkboxes above. Gear faults appear at gear-mesh frequency with sidebands; belt faults below 1×.

The bearing pedestal has a natural frequency. Sweep the speed through it and watch the 1× amplitude peak while the phase swings through 90°. Change the pedestal stiffness to move it; use the bump test to find it with the machine stopped.

    Why the spectrum is the diagnostic tool

    Every fault forces the machine at frequencies tied to its geometry: unbalance at 1× running speed, misalignment at 1× and 2×, looseness at many harmonics, bearing defects at non-integer multiples set by ball count and diameters, gears at teeth × speed. The FFT sorts the waveform into those frequencies so the source can be read off.

    Phase separates the look-alikes

    Unbalance, bent shaft and misalignment can all put energy at 1×. Phase across the coupling (misalignment: 180° apart), between shaft ends axially (bent shaft: 180°), or between horizontal and vertical (unbalance: about 90°) tells them apart. The tachometer gives the reference.

    Bearing frequencies (6205, 9 balls)

    BPFO = 3.58 ×  ·  BPFI = 5.42 ×  ·  BSF = 2.36 ×  ·  FTF = 0.40 ×

    Early bearing damage is tiny in velocity terms but each impact rings the bearing at a few kilohertz. Band-pass the acceleration around that ring, take the envelope, and the repetition rate of the impacts appears as clean lines. Inner-race and ball defects carry 1× or cage sidebands because the defect moves in and out of the load zone.

    Resonance

    |H| = 1/√((1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²)  ·  r = f/fn

    A structural natural frequency amplifies whatever forcing sits near it. Sweep the speed: amplitude peaks and the phase lags by 90° at fn. A bump test with the machine stopped rings the same frequency directly.

    ISO 10816-3 zones (group 2, rigid foundation)

    A ≤ 1.4  ·  B ≤ 2.8  ·  C ≤ 4.5  ·  D > 4.5 mm/s RMS

    Overall level says how bad; the spectrum says why. Both are needed.

    About the rig data

    [Illustrative rig data, for training only.] 4-pole motor, flexible coupling, 40 mm shaft on two 6205 bearings with one or two 3 kg discs, optional 23 : 57 gearbox and 120 : 240 mm belt drive. Sensor sensitivities, resonance frequencies and fault amplitudes are representative, not measured.

    How to use the simulator

    • Manufacture a fault in the panels under the machine: add a mass to the disc, mis-shim the motor, loosen a bearing, fit a damaged bearing, damage a gear or belt, or move the pedestal resonance.
    • Measure it: pick a point and direction, read the overall level and ISO zone, and read the spectrum, waveform, envelope spectrum and the 1× phase table. Everything is computed from a synthesised acceleration signal, so peaks appear only when the machine actually produces them.
    • Order axis shows the spectrum in multiples of running speed, which is how faults are read; switch it off for hertz. Fault-frequency cursors are off by default so learners identify lines themselves.
    • Record readings on a route and export them. Blind machine hides a random fault for diagnosis.
